Like mattresses, pillows play a major role in how well you sleep — and finding the right one can be challenging. Your sleeping position and weight can all affect how your pillow feels. People with neck and shoulder pain may need a pillow that’s ergonomically shaped, while heavy snorers often require a high-loft pillow to keep their heads upright. 

Our team evaluates factors like shape retention, moldability, temperature control, and odor potential when testing pillows in our lab. We also look at each pillow’s density, firmness options, and ease of cleaning. We based our picks below on our testers’ scores along with in-depth research and analysis.

Why Do I Need a New Pillow?

Maybe you’re one of the lucky ones who swears by your current pillow. Be that as it may, it’s not a bad time to start thinking about your next one. Whereas a mattress can have a lifespan of a decade, you really should swap out your pillow every couple years. Why? Even with a pillow case (you are using a pillow case, righhhtt?) your pillow absorbs all sorts of unpleasantries (hello dust mites, skin flakes, and plenty of sweat).

If that isn’t enough to convince you, we should also note that pillows wear out over time, even if that change has been too gradual for you to notice. What was once a high-loft skyscraper will eventually be whittled down to a sad pile of lumpy puffs. 

And given that mattresses are so expensive and pillows are so important, pillows are one of the most accessible entry points into improving your sleep system.

They’re the best bang for your buck.

Choosing Your Perfect Pillow

Finding the perfect pillow for you comes down to your sleeping position and personal preferences. The best way to zero in on the right choice is trial and error, which means sleeping on a wide variety of different types until you find your match. 

In an ideal world, you would be able to go to a store and lay on a mattress with whatever pillows you want to try. Since trying dozens of different pillows isn’t always realistic, here are a few things to consider when shopping.

You want your pillow to fill the gap between your head and neck based on how you sleep (back, side, stomach, or a combo of all three) to keep your neck aligned with the rest of your spine.

The pillow should meet your head where it naturally rests and have some compression, but it also needs to be supportive, firm, and comfortable enough for a good night’s sleep. 

It can be hard to find that perfect all-in-one pillow, so building your own pillow system can be a good compromise. “My daily driver is a shredded foam pillow. It’s comfortable five nights out of the week. If I need a firmer pillow, I have another that I interchange,” Sleep Foundation Test Lab Executive Product Director Jackson Lindeke says.

What is Loft?

Loft refers to the height of the pillow – not necessarily the firmness – and it’s the interaction of firmness and loft that is crucial. Ultimately, you need to ask yourself: Does it feel right?

For example, a high-loft down pillow might be so soft that it compresses all the way to the mattress, or a thin pillow like buckwheat might be so hard that it doesn’t move unless you do. 

Generally, you’ll see options for low, medium, and high loft, and each of these will complement different sleeping positions and needs. A low loft pillow measures 3 inches or less in height. A medium loft pillow measures between 3 and 5 inches thick. A high loft pillow measures over 5 inches in height.

Low-loft provides a thinner pillow profile. It’s a good choice for stomach sleepers since it keeps the angle of the head and neck low and aligned with the spine.

“Side sleepers tend to need a high-loft pillow for support and to fill the space beneath the head and neck to promote proper spinal alignment. Keep your head and neck in alignment at around 30 degrees with the help of a good pillow so your airway stays open during the night, allowing air to flow from your nose to your lungs without obstruction.”

– Sarah Silverman, Psy.D., a licensed psychologist and holistic sleep wellness consultant who treats patients in New York and Florida.

Mid-loft provides more elevation, and is usually suitable for back sleepers, side sleepers, and those who switch between positions at night.

Sleepers who snore or who suffer from acid reflux often need additional elevation and can benefit from a high-loft pillow to keep their head more upright.

What Kinds of Fills Are There?

Fill refers to the material your pillow is filled with. There are many different types of fill, and each contributes to a different feel and firmness level.

Next we’ll discuss some of the most common fills you’ll encounter while pillow shopping, as well as what to expect from each. 

Down 

Down refers to the inner plumage of ducks and geese (the clusters typically found on the belly or chest of the bird). It’s soft, lightweight, and particularly moldable. The result is a plush feel that sinks around your neck and head. 

As Senior Product Expert Jeremy Klein describes it, down has “that luxury hotel feel to it. There’s a lot of airflow and it has a light, clean feeling. It feels fresh in a way foam or latex does not. It’s very much its own feeling.”

While some sleepers may enjoy the cloud-like luxury of down, for others, it may not be firm enough.

“On average, down is one of the least supportive materials,” Lindeke explains.

To get around this, some manufacturers either overstuff their pillows, or place down around a more traditional feather core.

For allergy sufferers, having an allergy to down can be a very real thing, but it’s more common to have an allergy to dust mites, which can live in either down or down alternative. So if you’re actually allergic to dust mites, you’re not avoiding them if you go with a down alternative over down. 

Feather

Feather fills use the outer plumage of ducks and geese and tend to be less expensive than down. While the fill comes from the same source as down, there are some key differences.

“While down comes from a cluster of soft, close-to-the body feathers, the downside of exterior feathers is that they can tend to poke you,” Lindeke says. 

Feathers typically come from the bird’s wings and back and are much more rigid than down since their purpose is to protect the bird from weather and predators and help with flight. 

Down Alternative

A lot of “down” pillows are really down alternative. What people mean by down alternative isn’t consistent, so it’s good to check your labels. 

Also known as polyester fiberfill or polyfill, this is the most prevalent of all pillow types on the market due to its cost, ease of maintenance, and wide availability. 

Polyfill attempts to mimic the feel of down, but has some nuance. “Down alternative feels kind of slippery,” Klein says. “There’s less friction when you rub it, where down seems to catch against itself.”

Down alternative is a viable choice if you’re looking for a pillow that’s machine washable, doesn’t use animal products, and is inexpensive.

“Down alternative is definitely the more accessible price point for most people,” Lindeke says. “If you go to a big box store, the majority of those pillows are down alternative.”

Memory Foam

Memory foam is made from polyurethane and compresses in response to heat and pressure, providing a supportive cradle for your head and neck.

“Pressure relief is probably the biggest benefit of memory foam,” Klein says. “I have a lofty shredded foam pillow that I sleep on, which is funny because, in my head, I prefer my down pillow. When I think of foam pillows, I think of them being kind of sweaty, but for whatever reason, I just get better support and relief on this. With my down pillow, my head sinks in too much, and I end up grabbing another pillow.”

Foam pillows come in blocks or as shreds encased in a cover. Since foam hugs closer to your head, you can expect memory foam to sleep warmer than other materials. To help combat heat retention, some companies engineer their foam with tiny air bubbles or add cooling gel or copper.

These pillows might not retain the shape that you need to fill the gap between your head and shoulder, so if you can’t live without memory foam, this is where your mattress-to-pillow interaction can really make or break your sleep system. 

As an aside, it’s worth noting that memory foam (and polyfoam) pillows do have a “new foam smell” upon unboxing, but it does dissipate after a couple days. 

Latex

If you’re a hot sleeper or an eco-conscious consumer, there’s a lot to love about latex. Natural latex is derived from the sap of rubber trees and is breathable, durable, and resistant to mites and mildew. It’s also at the higher end, price-wise, of pillow materials. 

Like foam, latex pillows usually come in block or shredded form, and both have a buoyancy that’s airy and supportive. Latex is more breathable than other foams and sometimes even more breathable than down.

Latex doesn’t have the same compression as memory foam because it’s less reactive to heat. It maintains bounce and firmness, and your head isn’t going to sink in over time.

“Solid latex is great for side sleepers and back sleepers,” Klein says. “It might be a bit too thick for stomach sleepers, but if you want latex, go for shredded and take a bunch of the shreds out until it’s nice and thin.”

Buckwheat

This brings us to our bonus fill.

Buckwheat hulls feel firm individually, but are small enough to comfortably conform to the shape of your head and neck – similar to a bean bag. 

“It’s very moldable. Once you get a buckwheat pillow to how you want it, it will stay like that,” Lindeke says. “It’s definitely very firm, which is good for a side or back sleeper, and they’re also great at temperature regulation, so they’re good for hot sleepers”

Since the buckwheat hulls on the inside are replaceable over time, when they wear down, you can just buy more to replace them and toss or compost the old filling.

One buckwheat quirk: If you’re sensitive to sound and you change positions throughout the night, it will make noise and be quite annoying. Buckwheat also has a grainy smell, like a rye or stout, which may be comforting to some and obnoxious to others. 

Adjustable Fill 

If you’re paralyzed by choice, and don’t want to play a guessing game over whether or not firm means the same thing to you as it does to the manufacturer, adjustable and multi-chambered pillows are also a worthwhile option that you can find with most fill types. 

They come super full, and you can remove and add back filling until you create your perfect pillow.

“Layered pillars are a newer phenomenon with a legitimate niche in the market. They’re configurable, and you can interchange the layers to find the right comfort system,” Lindeke says.

How We Test: Rigor, Data, and Sleeper Feedback

Every sleeper has unique needs and preferences when it comes to choosing the best pillow for them. Our team of experts personally evaluates each pillow in our Seattle-based Test Lab. Each pillow undergoes the same hands-on process, which involves testing across different performance categories, including conforming, support, temperature regulation, and off-gassing. We also consider firmness, ease of care, and pricing when assigning ratings. Our test team is comprised of sleepers with various sleep preferences and body types to ensure that our testing represents a wide array of sleepers. Guidelines for our pillow testing system are listed below.

Construction analysis: Our team carefully reviews the loft, firmness, and material composition of each pillow. This helps us determine overall quality, expected lifespan, and ease of care.

Product testing: We take turns lying on each pillow in different sleep positions — this helps us determine whether it is best suited to side, back, stomach, or combination sleeping. We also hold and handle the pillow to measure qualities like temperature control, shape retention, and moldability.

Field testing: In addition to evaluations in our Seattle Test Lab, we assign performance ratings to pillows based on experiences of our field testers who use the pillows in their own homes. Field test data is collected over the course of several weeks to ensure accurate results.

User testing: We use feedback from real pillow owners collected from surveys and product reviews. This real-world data gives us insights into how each pillow performs over time, and whether our initial ratings reflect owner experiences.
