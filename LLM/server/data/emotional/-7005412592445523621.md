How Are You Viewed by Others at Work?
The psychology of interpersonal impression formation.
Posted
April 14, 2025
|
Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.
Share
Tweet
Share on Bluesky
Share
Email
Key points
Humans automatically form impressions of each other within the first seconds of meeting.
Psychology has documented several ways that our impression formation is biased.
Understanding the psychology of impression formation allows the opportutnity for impression management.
Humans are wired to automatically notice cues about another person in an attempt to figure out what to expect. When we meet someone new, we begin forming an impression of that person within seconds. Of course, as we gain greater experience with that other person, we revise our impression of them accordingly. However, the process of interpersonal impression formation is not nearly as simple and direct as that. The result is that our views of each other are distorted.
Why People’s Views of You Are Inaccurate
You’ve probably heard the old adage that first impressions are important. Indeed, the phenomenon is known in psychology as “
anchoring
.” When we’re first presented with any bit of information, we latch onto it as our starting point for forming an impression. We do adjust that impression as we gain more information, but that initial nugget exerts an over-sized effect, sort of anchoring us to that first impression.
If anchoring wasn’t bad enough, we automatically experience several types of
bias
in how we interpret additional information we collect about another person. These biases are baked into the way our brains work, so we’re not even aware of them. One of them is
confirmation bias
– the tendency to notice and remember information that fits with our beliefs, and ignore and not remember information that clashes. When it comes to impressions of another person, our initial impression becomes a sort of lens through which we view that person, so we notice and remember information that confirms our initial judgment. With time, it feels like we were right all along about what that other person is like.
Another baked-in bias has to do with a sort of double standard we have when it comes to negative behavior. In general when we notice another person doing something wrong, like being late or ignoring our “hello,” we tend to assume the reason is related to a character flaw. They were late because they are disorganized, irresponsible, or disrespectful of other people’s time. They didn’t respond to our greeting because they’re rude or self-absorbed. The double standard is that when we do the same types of things, we know the circumstances that led us to act that way, so we don’t think of ourselves as inherently bad people. We were late because of a good reason that was not within our control, and we didn’t notice the other person’s greeting because we were distracted.
Another source of distortion has to do with unequal sources of information when it comes to ourselves versus others. When we do something, we are aware of our thoughts, feelings, and intentions. However, when others see what we do, they only have access to what they observe and the effect our behavior has on them. So when our behavior has a negative effect on someone, that person is much more likely than us to assume that our behavior came from a place of uncaring, selfishness, or disrespect.
Putting It All Together
Now imagine how the few phenomena described here blend together to result in your reputation at work. As you first meet someone, they quickly form an initial impression of the kind of person you seem to be. You continue to get to know each other, but confirmation bias leads them to reinforce their initial assessment of you, so over time it comes to feel more “right.” If they initially liked you, their biased lens is a positive one, and they are liable to cut you some slack when you’re not at your best. If their initial impression of you was less than rosy, then when you happen to engage in a negative behavior it is more likely to be attributed to unflattering
personality
traits or undesirable motives.
Of course, you experience the same process when you get to know others, and just like them, you’re unaware of the distortions that are baked into the way you make sense of the social landscape.
What Can You Do About It?
To cultivate and preserve your reputation, it’s important to remember the primacy of first impressions. That is, be intentional in how you act when you first meet someone. If circumstances result in your less-than-best self during a first meeting, be intentional in explaining how and why that is the case. “I’m sorry if I’m coming across as negative. I’m not normally like this, but I’m having an unusually bad day.” The goal is to prevent a negative first impression, and when it occurs, attempt to correct it as soon as possible.
Because one cause of inaccurate perceptions about you is that others lack access to your thoughts and intentions, it’s important to share those when you can. We tend to assume that other people know how we think and what we intend with our actions, but that isn’t actually the case. Especially when your actions result in unwanted consequences for others, it’s important to express that that was not your intention. “I’m sorry that my remark to the boss got you in trouble. That was not my intention, and I had no idea it would be taken that way.”
Similarly, when you realize that you’ve demonstrated a negative behavior, such as arriving late or possibly having an angry facial expression when talking with someone, it’s important to provide a context or rationale for it. Otherwise, people might automatically attribute your behavior to a flawed character. “I’m sorry I’m late. Traffic was much heavier than usual.” “I’m sorry if I appear angry. It’s not you, I’m just feeling frustrated over the situation.”

In the end, we’re talking about being intentional in your
impression management
. The term “impression
management
” may conjure images of being fake in an attempt to come across in ways other than your real self. Here I’m talking about the opposite—being conscious of the psychology of impression formation and attempting to ensure that others form accurate impressions of you. Rather than
deception
, this goal requires a good dose of self-awareness and genuine transparency.
