How to Rest Without Guilt
Workaholism can lead to dissatisfaction, anxiety, and unhappiness.

April 14, 2025
|
Reviewed by Tyler Woods

Key points
Rest is not a waste of time—it is necessary step toward making success sustainable.
Workaholism is frequently rewarded at work, but can lead to burnout, stress, and anxiety.
Scheduling times for active rest keeps you engaged and restores your energy.
Source: Joseph Frank / Unsplash
The pressure to be constantly productive and efficient can feel inescapable—especially for high-achieving professionals.
I have worked with many people who have had highly successful careers, but the primary issue is not efficiency or effectiveness, but rather knowing
how and when to stop working—how to slow down and rest.
For many, rest can feel like a luxury you can't afford.
Productivity
can become an
addiction
.
The relentless pursuit of productivity can lead to diminishing returns,
burnout
, and cognitive fatigue. Our obsession with doing more can ultimately undermine our ability to connect and spend time in ways we find more deeply rewarding.
So why is it so hard to step away and rest without
guilt
?
The Psychology Behind Workaholism
An unhealthy preoccupation with productivity is not just about working long hours—it is about tying your
self-worth
and sense of safety to your achievements. This is contingent self-worth,
where your sense of value depends on external accomplishments.
For high achievers, this often starts early. Praise and recognition, often from parents or authority figures, were tied to performance, reinforcing the belief that "If I am not achieving, I'm not valuable." Over time, this mindset creates a compulsive need to be busy. Workaholism has been linked to increased
anxiety
,
depression
, a weaker immune system, and cardiovascular disease.
Granted, this trait can simultaneously be a strength and is frequently rewarded at work.
However, without active rest, the body can be pushed past its limit. When you are running at 200 percent for most of your
career
, people in their late 30s and 40s can start to experience physical issues: weight gain, chest tension, back pain,
migraines
, body aches, exhaustion, and being more prone to physical injuries or colds. Anxiety and rumination stick around longer, disrupting sleep and mood.
It is no coincidence that
researchers
at Stanford have found two accelerated "bursts" of aging, at age 44 and 60, that catch many workaholics off guard.
The Fear of “Wasted” Time
Resting can feel uncomfortable when your brain equates productivity with safety.
Focusing on work often allows people to avoid dealing with more difficult feelings and realities of their lives, such as grieving, sadness, or lack of meaning or purpose.
Neuroscience
research
shows that people who have an overactive
default mode network
(DMN)—the part of the brain responsible for self-reflection, future planning, and resting—are less happy. When we finally take a break, instead of feeling relaxed, our mind floods with anxious thoughts about what we
should
be doing.

This discomfort leads many people to “fake rest”—scrolling emails, multitasking, or zoning out on
social media
— all in an effort to avoid true downtime. But this kind of fragmented rest does little to restore the mind.
The Science of Rest and Why It’s Essential
True rest restores cognitive and emotional recovery. Studies show that strategic rest can:
Enhance memory and problem-solving.
The brain consolidates learning and strengthens neural connections during downtime.
Improve
emotional regulation
.
Breaks reduce cortisol levels, helping regulate
stress
and prevent emotional exhaustion and irritability.
Boost
creativity
.
Rest activates the default mode network in a way that cultivates insight and innovative thinking.
Increase productivity.
The ultradian rhythm theory suggests that our brains function optimally in 90-minute cycles, after which we need a break to maintain
peak performance
.

The Problem With Guilt
Despite these benefits, many high achievers struggle to rest because of “productivity guilt”—the nagging feeling that they should be doing more, constantly.
How to Unlearn Productivity Guilt and Embrace Rest
1. Reframe Rest as an Investment, Not a Reward
One of the biggest mindset shifts is recognizing that rest is not something you "earn" after reaching exhaustion—it's a prerequisite for sustained success. Just as elite athletes build rest days into their training, high performers must incorporate recovery time to maintain peak cognitive function.
Action plan:
Invest in connections. Make time for those small moments of joy.
Instead of seeing rest as a break
from
productivity, reframe it as a tool
for
productivity. “Resting now will help me think more clearly, make better decisions, and work more effectively later.”
2. Practice “Active Rest”
Research shows that flow states, achieved through creativity, exercise, or brainstorming, enhance cognitive flexibility and focus.
Action plan:
Schedule at least one hour of active rest per week doing something enjoyable that is not work-related.
3. Set
Boundaries
Around Work and Rest
Setting firm boundaries can help separate work from leisure, preventing chronic stress.
Action plan:
Use a “shutdown ritual.”
At the end of your workday, write down unfinished tasks for tomorrow, close your laptop, and engage in a transition activity (like taking a walk).
Time-block rest.
Schedule downtime in your calendar just as you would a work meeting.
4. Challenge the
Inner Critic
If you feel guilty about resting, ask yourself: “Where did I learn that my worth is based on my productivity?”
Action plan:
Psychotherapy
can help you explore and rewrite themes from
childhood
and early experiences that may have lead to workaholism as a way of coping, while still preserving your ambition.
5. Prioritize Sleep as a Non-Negotiable
Lack of sleep is a badge of honor for many high performers, but sleep deprivation impairs
decision-making
, emotional regulation, and
long-term memory
. Prioritizing sleep is one of the simplest ways to improve mental clarity and
resilience
.
Action plan:
Establish a consistent
bedtime
sleep routine
. Include a 10- to 15-minute body scan guided mediation and restorative yoga poses like legs-up-the-wall pose.
Set aside your phone
. Avoid checking work emails before bed.
Sustainable Success Without Sacrificing Well-Being
You don’t have to choose between ambition and self-care. The most effective professionals are not those who grind themselves down, but those who master the art of strategic rest.
You can break free from productivity addiction by remembering: Rest is not a waste of time—it is necessary step toward making success sustainable.
