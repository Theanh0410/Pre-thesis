Family Dysfunction at the Macro Level
Family dysfunction leaves a long shadow that may lead to later retraumatization.
Posted
April 14, 2025

Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

Key points
Dysfunctional childhoods affect people well into adulthood.
The roles we learn as we grow up are often the roles we play in the workplace.
Growing up with an unpredictable parent can be traumatizing, and unpredictable leaders can be retraumatizing.
If you grew up in a family in which a parent or
caregiver
suffered from
addiction
, mental illness, or a
personality disorder
, there’s a good chance that you consider yourself to have grown up in a dysfunctional family. While a lot of us feel that every family is a little dysfunctional in some way, markedly different are the families that are marred by these disorders.
One aspect of Family Systems Theory, which was developed by psychiatrist Murray Bowen, is that we often recapitulate our family dynamics in the workplace. This means that we tend to play out the roles we learned in our families once we enter the workforce. For instance, if you’re the family’s hero, you’ll also want to shine on the job. Or, if you grew up in a dysfunctional family, you might unconsciously seek out a workplace that’s a little dysfunctional—or you might bring in the dysfunction yourself. This allows you to play out the role you’ve spent your whole life learning.
Dysfunctional families have been described as having particular role players baked into the family system. Wegscheider-Cruse listed the roles as heroes, scapegoats, mascots, and enablers. While it may be debatable whether these roles are played out in every, or even most, dysfunctional families, one thing virtually all have in common is a high level of unpredictability. Family members walk on eggshells because they don’t know how a parent is going to show up—angry, indulgent, taunting, punishing, or demanding. This persistent absence of stability and predictability shapes the family system and influences children’s development in lasting ways.
When a leader is unpredictable, the lack of stability can disrupt
productivity
,
self-confidence
, and the sense of security people need to feel safe. Unpredictable leaders diminish trust in the system and stoke
fear
of what might happen next. There are many ways to lead, but the
leadership
traits that people value most are consistently found to be honesty, open-mindedness, and compassion (Ponce de Leon & Bailey, 2025). People crave stability and certainty, but erratic behavior in a leader can lead to uncertainty and
anxiety
(Dmitri & Kumar, 2025). Erratic or capricious behavior by a supervisor diminishes a person’s desire to remain on the job. (For many who grew up in dysfunctional families, the current national unpredictable climate may feel too familiar, and they may feel trapped in another dysfunctional system.)
People need to feel that their leaders have their best interests at heart. If that’s not the vibe you’re getting, here are some ways to handle the shakiness or distress you’re experiencing:
Build a healthy support network.
Engage with others in healthy ways that focus on advocacy rather than just sharing complaints.
Seek out like-minded others in spaces where security and compassion are found.
Voice your concerns or thoughts in ways that will be heard—phone calls, letters, emails, peaceful demonstrations.
Be strategic in how you engage with those who don’t share your views.
Don’t put your safety at risk.
Remember that the best self-care is healthy
boundaries
.
