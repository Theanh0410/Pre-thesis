Is Time Packaged the Same Way Across Cultures?
How we partition the minutes in an hour may affect punctuality.
Posted
April 14, 2025
|
Reviewed by Lybi Ma
Share
Tweet
Share on Bluesky
Share
Email
Key points
Perceptions of lateness differ, Moroccans are less strict on punctuality than Americans.
Cultural backgrounds shape the definition of what it means to be on time.
Americans might divide time narrowly, while Arabs might use broader intervals.
Cultural anthropologists tell us that perceptions of time vary across cultural groups. Some societies are said to be monochronic (focusing on one task at a time) while others are polychronic (engaging in multiple activities simultaneously). Some societies are said to view time as linear (moving forward like an arrow) while others view time as cyclical (with repeating patterns).
Much less is known about a related topic—how cultures segment time. Do people in different cultures package units of time differently? Is one hour on the clock considered a series of 60 one-minute packets, a collection of twelve five-minute packets, or something else?
In his fascinating book
A Geography of Time
(1997), social psychologist Robert Levine observed that most Americans assess punctuality in terms of five-minute intervals, whereas most Arabs assess punctuality in terms of 15-minute intervals. An American, for example, might say someone was five minutes late; to say six minutes would sound strange. An Arab, on the other hand, might say someone was a quarter-hour late; to say 20 minutes would sound strange. At least that’s what Levine claimed.
The eminent anthropologist Edward T. Hall made a similar observation in his book
The Silent Language
(1959/1981). For most Americans, according to Hall, “There are eight time sets in regards to punctuality and length of appointments: on time, five, 10, 15, 20, thirty, 45 minutes, and one hour early or late.” The Mediterranean Arab, however, “makes fewer distinctions than we do. His scale has only three discernible points … no time at all; now (or present), which is of varying duration; and forever (too long).”
Although Levine and Hall both remarked that Americans divide time more narrowly and more precisely than Arabs, they presented no empirical evidence to support their claims. Spurred by these assertions, Raivo Valk, Abdessamad Dialmy, and I investigated the psychology of time segmentation among university students in Estonia, Morocco, and the United States (White, Valk, and Dialmy, 2011).
Time Segmentation in Three Different Countries
We asked 301 participants to complete a time-estimation task. On a signal from the experimenter, participants began to inspect an intricate diagram. After precisely 47 seconds, the experimenter told participants to set the diagram aside and estimate how long (in seconds) they had inspected the diagram. Almost everyone overestimated the amount of time that had elapsed, sometimes by a factor of 2 or 3.
We counted the number of students who reported their estimate as a multiple of 5. A response of 45 seconds or 50 seconds, for example, would count as a multiple of 5, but a response of 47 seconds would not. The percentages of Americans, Estonians, and Moroccans who estimated the length of time as a multiple of 5 were 93 percent, 81 percent, and 74 percent, respectively. The cultural differences were not large, but they were statistically significant.
In a second task, we asked participants to read five scenarios about individuals arriving for an appointment of some sort. For each scenario, they indicated how late someone could arrive before the person was
inappropriately
late. About 60 percent of the Moroccans in our study said that someone who arrived 30 minutes after the appointed time was not inappropriately late. In sharp contrast, most of the American participants said 20 minutes was too late.
What about the segmentation of time? When considering the five scenarios, two-thirds of Moroccans expressed their answers as a multiple of 15 (30 or 45 minutes, for example). Fewer than half of Americans did the same. (Estonians responded much like Americans.)
Clock Time versus Psychological Time
In short, Americans in our study typically packaged time into blocks of five seconds or five minutes, while Moroccans packaged time into blocks of 15. This may explain, in part, why Moroccans and other Arabs are often less punctual than Americans. An American who arrives 10 minutes after the appointed time is late by two units of psychological time. A Moroccan who arrives 30 minutes (two quarters of an hour) after the appointed time is also late by two units of psychological time.
When struggling to understand why some people are less punctual than others, it may be
wise
to consider how people partition the minutes in an hour—and to acknowledge that people with different cultural backgrounds may have different understandings of what it means to be “on time.”
