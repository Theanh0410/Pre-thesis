How Do You Think About Your Children?
Rethinking how we describe, see, and support our kids.
Posted
April 14, 2025
|
Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Key points
When thinking about your child, start with what’s right, not just what’s wrong.
Your words influence how your children perceive themselves—select them thoughtfully.
Struggles are real, but they are not the whole story; every child is still growing and evolving.
When I meet with parents during assessments, I always begin with the same question:
"Tell me about your child."
It's open-ended on purpose. I want to hear what comes to mind first. Time and again, I've noticed that most parents start with what's difficult. They share their experiences of tantrums, arguments,
anxiety
, school struggles, defiance, and lack of focus. Their words flow quickly and with
emotion
—usually frustration, sometimes worry, and often exhaustion.
It makes sense. After all, they're sitting with me because something isn't working. They're looking for help, guidance, and perhaps even hope. When your child struggles, especially over time, it's easy to feel as if the problems define them. Your thoughts begin to loop around the challenges, and the good parts can get buried.
But here's the thing: How we talk about our children influences how we perceive them. Our perception shapes our responses, and over time, it also affects how they view themselves.
No one is pretending that things are easy. I know parents don't walk in with a list of complaints because they don't love their kids. They do—deeply. However, discussing what's good when all the energy is spent putting out fires is challenging. Still, that shift in focus—pausing to remember what's right, not just what's wrong—matters more than most people realize.
More Than the Hard Parts
We live in a culture of fixing. When a child struggles, we zoom in on the symptoms and attempt to correct them. While some of this is necessary, when it becomes our only perspective, we risk reducing a complex and developing human to merely a collection of deficits.
I view it this way: When your child reaches adulthood, no one will inquire if they made their bed every morning in eighth grade. No one will assess their value based on how often they forget their homework or refuse to eat vegetables. People will want to know who they are and what they care about, what they excel at, and how they treat others—the kind of person they have become.
That
identity
doesn't grow from a constant focus on problems. It grows when kids are seen as more than their worst moments.
I've discovered that even amid profound struggle, there is always goodness to notice: quiet kindness, a
sense of humor
, intense curiosity, a creative mind, and a fierce sense of justice. It's all present—sometimes hidden, but never absent. When parents begin naming these traits aloud, something shifts. The conversations become lighter. The child feels human once again.
This isn't about ignoring behavior.
Boundaries
, structure, and accountability still matter, but they have a greater impact when they stem from a place of connection rather than correction alone. Children respond best when they feel recognized in the fullness of who they are.
I also consider how children perceive the way their parents describe them. This language serves as a mirror. If all they hear is, "He's impossible" or "She's lazy," those labels stick. Over time, they may stop trying to be anything else. However, if what they hear is, "He's persistent," "She feels things deeply," or "He's still figuring it out, but he has a good heart," then there's room to grow.
A New Way to See
If you're reading this and thinking, "But we're struggling right now," I understand. You wouldn't be here if everything were going smoothly. However, despite the struggle, there's an opportunity to shift your perspective. A change in mindset doesn't erase problems, but it enables you to confront them with greater clarity, compassion, and
resilience
.

Here are three simple ways to start:
First, when you think about your child, start by naming one thing you genuinely appreciate about them—not what they do, but who they are as a person. Focus on the positive, even if it feels small.
Second, pay
attention
to how you speak about your child to others. Replace judgment with curiosity. Instead of saying, "She never listens," consider saying, "She's strong-willed, and we're exploring how to work with that." This keeps the door open.
Finally, remind yourself—daily if needed—that your child is still developing. They are a work in progress. The challenges are real, but they do not define the entire narrative.
You can accept two truths simultaneously: that things are challenging right now and that your child possesses value, beauty, and potential. They are growing. And so are you.
