When to Trust Your Gut and When to Think Twice
Gut feelings are powerful but not foolproof. Learn when to trust your instincts.
Posted
April 14, 2025
|
Reviewed by Davia Sills
Share
Tweet
Share on Bluesky
Share
Email
Key points
Intuition stems from past experiences and pattern recognition, not just sudden insight.
Gut feelings work best when paired with facts, especially in high-stakes decisions.
Emotions can cloud intuition—pause before trusting the gut in emotionally charged moments.
The decision to trust our gut feeling is easy if we are reaching into a bowl to choose a ticket for a door prize. Since we can’t lean on expertise, practice, or information, we move our hands around until a ticket “feels” right. We trust our gut. Besides, if we choose wrong, there are few, if any, consequences.
But suppose the decision has important consequences, and we have time to analyze our options—like choosing a business partner or life partner, starting or stopping fertility treatment, or leaving a job for a new one. Should we trust our gut or think twice?
Both, according to research findings.
Gut feelings
may seem like they come from thin air and are suddenly just there, but cognitive
neuroscience
research tells us that these feelings come from
pattern recognition
and past experience. Apparently, our brains constantly collect and organize information to use when we have to make rapid decisions and send messages when they are needed to the neurons lining our gut, which is sometimes called the “second brain.” We may actually feel the messages as physical sensations in the pit of our stomach, warm flushes across our chest, or cold chills up our back.
What we call “intuition,” then, is really part of an early alert system to help us avoid danger and steer us toward safety. It gives us our “first impressions,” “premonitions,” “unexplained
anxiety
,” “hunches,” “sixth sense,” and “good vibes.”
But are our instincts always right, and do they give us enough information for big decisions?
My gut feeling is that your answer would be “probably not,” and you’d be right. Gut feelings often give us more information about ourselves and our own past experiences than they do about our current situation. So, as the stakes of a decision become higher, so should the amount of information we collect beyond our immediate hunch or
first impression
.
Suppose, for example, you are choosing a fertility practice. You not only want a clinic where you feel welcome and comfortable; you also want excellent outcomes. Both objective and subjective criteria count.
The same is true when we choose a therapist, a trainer, or a doula. They are relationships that should “feel right,” and our brain can identify
microexpressions
and nonverbal cues in two seconds, but we will also need credentials and references.
But suppose you’re selecting an accountant, a stockbroker, or a surgeon. Their performance record may not only be more important to us than our first impression; it may even override a negative first impression.
And then there are gut feelings that may be misleading.
Hit the pause button if your intuition is about a very emotional decision. When emotions are high, it is easier to confuse people from the past with those in the present, and instead of reading the room accurately, we may be practicing wishful thinking.
Don’t trust your
distrust
if you are making a decision under pressure. You may let anxiety or fears about the future create negative first impressions and gut feelings in the present. Have you ever felt “bad vibes” from someone you later learned you really liked? I have.
If you are over-analyzing a decision or conflicted about a situation, relying on your gut feeling can give you some relief, but that’s all it is—a quick fix. Gut feelings are starting points, not final answers, so don’t force the big decisions or give your intuition too big a job.
When a situation feels familiar, and your sixth sense tells you that you’ve been through this before, beware. You are more likely to have your intuition set on automatic pilot and miss clues and cues that could help you navigate this new situation.
So, when you have a “hunch” or “suspicion” that something is not right, check it out. Your reactions are coming from your valuable past observations. And when your intuition or sixth sense tells you something is just right, double-check also, just to be sure. Understanding how your gut feelings operate can help you value them and use them wisely.
